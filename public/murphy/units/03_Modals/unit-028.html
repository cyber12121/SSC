Unit 28 • Tenses Basics

must and can'tDeduction and certainty (present and past)

Learn how to make deductions using must for certainty and can't for impossibility, in both present and past forms.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Use must when you are certain something is true.
  • Use can't when you are certain something is impossible (opposite of must).
  • Form deductions about the past with must have (done) and can't have (done).

Part A — Theory

A. must and can't for deduction (Present)

We use must to say that we believe something is certain:

  • You've been traveling all day. You must be tired.
  • 'Celia is 80.' 'She must be joking! She doesn't look more than 60.'
  • The restaurant is always full. It must be very good.

We use can't to say that we believe something is impossible (opposite of must):

  • You've just had lunch. You can't be hungry already!
  • They haven't lived here very long. They can't know many people.

B. must have done and can't have done (Past)

For the past we use must have (done) and can't have (done):

• There's nobody at home. They must have gone out.

• I lost my glove. I must have dropped it somewhere.

• Sarah hasn't contacted me. She can't have got my message. (or couldn't have got)

Summary & Quick Reference

Key Takeaways for Unit 28

Category Rule Correct Example
must (certainty) Certain something is true based on evidence You've been traveling all day. You must be tired.
can't (impossibility) Certain something is NOT true (opposite of must) You've just eaten. You can't be hungry already!
Past Deductions must have + participle / can't have + participle They must have gone out. She can't have got my message.
