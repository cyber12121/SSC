Unit 94 • Relative clauses

Relative clauses 3: whose/whom/wherePossessive whose, formal whom with prepositions, and spatial where

We use whose for possession instead of his/her/their. We use whom in formal contexts and after prepositions, and where for places.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Master whose for possession (replacing his/her/their: a widow whose husband is dead).
  • Use whom in formal English and after prepositions (with whom, to whom).
  • Use where to refer to places (the hotel where we stayed).
  • Use the day / the time / the year / the reason (with that / why or no pronoun).

Part A — Theory

Part A: Whose (Possession)

We use whose in relative clauses instead of his / her / their:

We helped some people whose car had broken down.
(their car had broken down)
A widow is a woman whose husband is dead.
(her husband is dead)
What’s the name of the man whose phone you borrowed?
I met a man who knows you.
(he knows you)
I met a man whose sister knows you.
(his sister knows you)

Part B: Whom (Formal / After Prepositions)

Whom is possible instead of who when it is the object of the verb (formal English):

The woman whom I wanted to see was away on holiday.
(In informal spoken English we usually say: The woman I wanted to see... or who I wanted to see...)

We also use whom directly after prepositions: to whom • with whom • for whom • about whom:

The people with whom I work are very nice. (more formal)
The people I work with are very nice. (everyday English)

Part C: Where (Places)

We use where in a relative clause to talk about a place:

The restaurant where we had dinner was near the airport.
A cemetery is a place where people are buried.
I live in a friendly village where everybody knows everybody else.

Compare: the hotel where we stayed  |  the hotel (that) we stayed at.

Part D: The Day / The Time / The Reason

We say the day / the year / the time (that) something happened:

Do you remember the day (that) we first met?
2009 was the year (that) Amanda got married.

We say the reason (why / that) something happened:

The reason (why / that) I’m calling you is to ask for advice.
