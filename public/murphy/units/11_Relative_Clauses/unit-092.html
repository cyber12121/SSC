Unit 92 • Relative clauses

Relative clauses 1: clauses with who/that/whichSubject relative clauses for people (who/that) and things (that/which)

A relative clause tells us which person or thing the speaker means. We use who or that for people, and that or which for things.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Understand what a relative clause is and how it tells us which person or thing the speaker means.
  • Use who (or that) for people (the woman who lives next door, someone who designs buildings).
  • Use that or which (never who) for things (the machine that/which was broken).
  • Avoid duplicating pronouns (the woman who lives next door, not *who she lives).
  • Contrast what (= the thing that) with that.

Part A — Theory

Part A: What is a Relative Clause?

A clause is a part of a sentence. A relative clause tells us which person or thing (or what kind of person or thing) the speaker means:

The woman who lives next door is a doctor.
(‘who lives next door’ tells us which woman)
We stayed at a hotel which had a swimming pool.
(‘which had a swimming pool’ tells us what kind of hotel)

Part B: Who / That for People

We use who in a relative clause when we are talking about people (not things):

An architect is someone who designs buildings.
What was the name of the person who called you?
Anyone who wants to apply for the job must do so by Friday.

You can also use that (instead of who) for people, but who is more usual:

The woman that lives next door is a doctor.

Warning: Do NOT use which for people: the driver who caused the accident (not the driver which).

Part C: That / Which for Things

When we talk about things, we use that or which (not who):

Where is the cheese that was in the fridge?
Where is the cheese which was in the fridge?
I don’t like stories that have unhappy endings. (not stories who have)

Part D: No Duplicate Pronouns; That vs What

Remember that in relative clauses we use who/that/which, not he/she/they/it:

I’ve never spoken to the woman who lives next door. (not who she lives next door)
The car that broke down has been towed away. (not that it broke down)

What means ‘the thing(s) that’. Compare what and that:

What happened was my fault. (= The thing that happened was my fault.)
Everything that happened was my fault. (not Everything what happened)
The machine that broke down is working again. (not The machine what broke down)
