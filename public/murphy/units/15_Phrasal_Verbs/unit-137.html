Unit 137 • Phrasal verbs

Phrasal verbs 1: IntroductionStructure, movement vs special meaning, object positioning

Phrasal verbs combine a verb with a particle such as in, out, on, off, up, down, away, back. Discover how they function and where objects go.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Understand what a phrasal verb is (a verb combined with a particle like in, out, on, off, up, down, away, back).
  • Distinguish between literal movement (get on, drive off, turn round) and idiomatic meaning (break down, find out, give up, get by).
  • Use three-word phrasal verbs followed by a preposition (look forward to, keep up with, run away from).
  • Master object positioning: nouns can go before or after the particle (turn the light on / turn on the light), but pronouns MUST go between the verb and particle (turn it on, never *turn on it).

Part A — Theory

Part A: Introduction — Literal Movement vs Idiomatic Meaning

We often use verbs with particles such as in, out, on, off, up, down, away, back, by, through, about, along, over, forward, round/around. These combinations are called phrasal verbs.

1. Literal Movement

We often use on, off, out etc. with verbs of physical movement:

The bus was full. We couldn’t get on.
A woman got into the car and drove off.
Sarah is leaving tomorrow and coming back on Saturday.
When I touched him on the shoulder, he turned round.

2. Special (Idiomatic) Meaning

Often the second word gives a special meaning to the verb that cannot be guessed from the parts alone:

Sorry I’m late. The car broke down. (= the engine stopped working)
I never found out who sent me the flowers. (= I never discovered)
It was my first flight. I was nervous as the plane took off. (= left the ground into the air)
I tried many times to contact her. In the end I gave up. (= stopped trying)
How was the exam? How did you get on? (= How did you do / manage?)
My French isn’t good, but it’s enough to get by. (= enough to manage)

Part B: Phrasal Verb + Preposition (Three-Word Verbs)

Sometimes a phrasal verb is followed by a preposition. The pattern is: verb + adverb particle + preposition:

We looked up at the plane as it flew above us.
Why did you run away from me?
You’re walking too fast. I can’t keep up with you.
Are you looking forward to your trip?

Part C: Position of the Object — The Pronoun Rule

When a phrasal verb has an object, there are two important rules:

1. When the object is a NOUN:

Usually both positions are correct — before or after the particle:

I turned on the light.  or  I turned the light on.
I’m going to take off my shoes.  or  I’m going to take my shoes off.
Don’t wake up the baby.  or  Don’t wake the baby up.
Don’t throw away this box.  or  Don’t throw this box away.

2. When the object is a PRONOUN (it / them / me / him / her / us):

There is ONLY ONE possible position — the pronoun MUST go between the verb and the particle:

I turned it on. (NOT *turned on it*)
These shoes are uncomfortable. I’m going to take them off. (NOT *take off them*)
The baby is asleep. Don’t wake her up. (NOT *wake up her*)
I want to keep this box, so don’t throw it away. (NOT *throw away it*)
