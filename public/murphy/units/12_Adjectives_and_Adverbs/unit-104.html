Unit 104 • Adjectives and adverbs

quite, pretty, rather and fairlydegrees of comparison: quite/pretty (= medium degree), rather (= negative/surprising), fairly (= weak); quite (= completely)

'Quite' and 'pretty' mean less than 'very' but more than 'a little'. 'Rather' is often used for negative or surprising ideas. 'Fairly' is weaker. 'Quite' also uniquely means 'completely'.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Use quite and pretty to express a moderate degree (between 'a little' and 'very').
  • Use rather for negative qualities (rather cloudy, rather shy) or surprising positive qualities (rather good).
  • Identify fairly as a weaker modifier (fairly good, fairly big).
  • Recognize the second meaning of quite: 'completely' with non-gradable adjectives (quite sure, quite right, quite impossible).

Part A — Theory

Part A: Quite and Pretty (Medium Degree)

Quite and pretty = less than 'very', but more than 'a little':

I'm surprised you haven't heard of her. She's quite famous.  or  She's pretty famous.
Anna lives quite near me, so we see each other pretty often.

Pretty is informal and used mainly in spoken English.

Word Order with a/an:

quite a/an + adjective + noun: Sarah has quite a good job. (not a quite good job)
a pretty + adjective + noun: Sarah has a pretty good job.

Special uses of quite (not pretty):

• quite a/an + noun: It was quite a surprise. (= a big surprise)
• quite a lot of …: There were quite a lot of guests.
• quite + verb: I quite like tennis, but it's not my favourite.

Part B: Rather

Rather is often used for negative ideas (things we think are not good):

The weather isn't so good. It's rather cloudy.
Paul is rather shy. He doesn't talk very much.

When used for positive ideas, rather means 'unusually' or 'surprisingly':

These oranges are rather good. Where did you get them? (= surprisingly good)

Part C: Fairly (Weaker)

Fairly is weaker than quite / rather / pretty:

My room is fairly big, but I'd prefer a bigger one.
We see each other fairly often, but not as often as we used to.

Part D: Quite = 'Completely'

Quite also means completely with non-gradable adjectives:

quite sure
quite right
quite true
quite clear
quite different
quite impossible
quite safe
quite obvious
‘Are you sure?’ ‘Yes, quite sure.’ (= completely sure)
She was quite different from what I expected.
Everything they said was quite true.
I quite agree with you. (= I completely agree)
Not quite = not completely: I don't quite understand.

The story is quite interesting.
(= less than very interesting)

The story is quite true.
(= completely true)
