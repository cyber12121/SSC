Unit 105 • Adjectives and adverbs

Comparative 1 (cheaper, more expensive etc.)-er for short adjectives & two-syllable -y; more for longer adjectives and -ly adverbs; irregular forms

We use the comparative form to compare two things. Short adjectives take -er, while longer adjectives and -ly adverbs take 'more'. Some adjectives have irregular comparative forms.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Form comparatives with -er for one-syllable adjectives (cheaper, faster, larger, thinner) and two-syllable adjectives ending in -y (luckier, easier, earlier).
  • Form comparatives with more … for words of two or more syllables (more serious, more expensive) and adverbs ending in -ly (more slowly).
  • Identify adjectives that accept both -er and more: clever, narrow, quiet, shallow, simple.
  • Master irregular comparatives: good/well → better, bad/badly → worse, far → further / farther.

Part A — Theory

Part A: Introduction to Comparatives

Look at these examples:

How shall we travel? Shall we drive or go by train?
Let's drive. It's cheaper.
Don't go by train. It's more expensive.

After comparatives you can use than:

It's cheaper to drive than go by train.
Going by train is more expensive than driving.

Part B: Formation Rules (-er vs More)

-er (short words)

One syllable:
cheap → cheaper • fast → faster
large → larger • thin → thinner

Two syllables ending in -y (-y → -ier):
lucky → luckier • early → earlier
easy → easier • pretty → prettier

The exam was easier than I expected.

more … (longer words)

Two syllables or more:
more serious • more expensive
more often • more comfortable

Adverbs ending in -ly:
more slowly • more seriously
more easily • more quietly

Can you walk a bit more slowly?

Dual forms (-er or more …):

clever • narrow • quiet • shallow • simple
It's too noisy here. Can we go somewhere quieter?  or  … somewhere more quiet?

Part C: Irregular Comparatives

good / well → better:
The garden looks better since you tidied it up.
I know him better than anybody else.

bad / badly → worse:
‘How's your headache? Better?’ ‘No, it's worse.’
He did worse in the exam than expected.

far → further (or farther):
It's a long walk to the park – further than I thought.
Note: further (not farther) also means 'additional': Let me know if you hear any further news.
