Unit 106 • Adjectives and adverbs

Comparative 2 (much better, any better etc.)modifiers with comparatives (much, a lot, far, slightly); any/no + comparative; better and better; the … the …

We use words like 'much', 'a lot', 'far', and 'slightly' before comparatives. We repeat comparatives (better and better) to show ongoing change, and use 'the … the …' to show proportionate change.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Modify comparatives using much, a lot, far, a bit, a little, slightly.
  • Use any and no with comparatives: any longer, no bigger than.
  • Express continuous change by repeating comparative forms: better and better, more and more expensive.
  • Master proportionate correlation: the … the … (the sooner the better, the more you use the higher your bill).
  • Distinguish between older (general) and elder (family members only, attributive).

Part A — Theory

Part A: Modifying Comparatives

Before comparative adjectives and adverbs you can use:

much • a lot • far (= a lot) • a bit • a little • slightly (= a little)
I felt ill earlier, but I feel much better now. (or a lot better)
Don't go by train. It's a lot more expensive.
Could you speak a bit more slowly?
This bag is slightly heavier than the other one.
The problem is far more serious than we thought.

Part B: Any / No + Comparative

You can use any and no + comparative (any longer, no bigger etc.):

I've waited long enough. I'm not waiting any longer. (= not even a little longer)
Their apartment is no bigger than ours. (or isn't any bigger than)
Do you feel any better?
This hotel is better, and it's no more expensive.

Part C: Better and Better / More and More (Continuous Change)

We repeat comparatives to express that something changes continuously:

Your English is improving. It's getting better and better.
The city has grown fast. It's got bigger and bigger.
I became more and more convinced that he was lying.
More and more tourists are visiting this part of the country.

Part D: The … the … (Proportionate Change)

We use the … the … to say that one thing depends on another:

‘What time shall we leave?’ ‘The sooner the better.’ (= as soon as possible)
‘What sort of bag do you want?’ ‘The bigger the better.’
The sooner we leave, the earlier we'll arrive.
The younger you are, the easier it is to learn.
The more electricity you use, the higher your bill will be.
The more I thought about it, the less I liked it.

Part E: Older vs Elder

The standard comparative of old is older:

David looks older than he really is. (not looks elder)

We use elder only before a noun for members of a family (my elder sister, their elder son):

My elder sister is a TV producer. (or My older sister …)
Note: We do not say 'somebody is elder':
My sister is older than me. (not elder than me)
