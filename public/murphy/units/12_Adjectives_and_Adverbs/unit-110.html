Unit 110 • Adjectives and adverbs

Word order 2: adverbs with the verbposition of adverbs (always, also, probably, never etc.); before main verb; after am/is/are/was/were; after auxiliary/first verb; both and all

Adverbs of frequency and opinion (always, usually, also, probably etc.) usually go in the middle of a sentence: before a single-word verb, after am/is/are/was/were, or after the first auxiliary verb.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Position adverbs of frequency and manner (always, also, probably, already) in front of single-word verbs (always drives).
  • Place adverbs after forms of be (am, is, are, was, were).
  • Place adverbs after the first auxiliary verb when verbs have two or more parts (can never remember, will probably be cancelled).
  • Correctly position all and both with the verb (We all felt ill, They were both born).
  • Position adverbs before have to (I always have to phone him) and before negative auxiliaries with probably (I probably won't see you).

Part A — Theory

Part A: Adverbs with Single-Word Verbs

Some adverbs (for example, always, also, probably, never, often, usually) go with the verb in the middle of a sentence.

If the verb is one word (drives / cooked / fell etc.), the adverb goes before the verb:

SubjectAdverbVerbRest of sentence
Emilyalwaysdrivesto work.
Ialmostfellas I was going down the stairs.
I cleaned the house and also cooked the dinner. (not cooked also)
Laura hardly ever watches television and rarely reads newspapers.
‘Shall I give you my address?’ ‘No, I already have it.’

Special rules for have to and be:

Before have to: Joe never phones me. I always have to phone him. (not I have always to phone)
After am / is / are / was / were: We were feeling very tired and we were also hungry. (not also were)
You're always late. You're never on time.
The traffic isn't usually as bad as it was this morning.

Part B: Adverbs with Two or More Verbs

If the verb is two or more words (for example, can remember / will be cancelled), the adverb usually goes after the first verb (can / doesn't / will etc.):

SubjectVerb 1AdverbVerb 2Rest
Icanneverrememberher name.
Claredoesn'tusuallydriveto work.
Are you—definitelygoingaway next week?
The meetingwillprobablybecancelled.
You've always been very kind to me.
Jack can't cook. He can't even boil an egg.
Do you still work for the same company?
The house was only built a year ago and it's already falling down.

Note on probably: probably goes before a negative auxiliary (isn't / won't etc.):

I probably won't see you. (or I'll probably not see you, but not I won't probably)

Part C: All and Both with the Verb

We also use all and both with the verb in the middle of a sentence (following the same position rules):

We all felt ill after the meal. (not felt all ill)
My parents are both teachers.
Sarah and Jane have both applied for the job.
My friends are all going out tonight.

Part D: Short Forms and Ellipsis (is / will / did etc.)

When we use is / will / did etc. alone instead of repeating part of a sentence, we put always / never etc. before the verb:

He always says he won't be late, but he always is. (= he is always late)
I've never done it and I never will. (= I will never do it)
