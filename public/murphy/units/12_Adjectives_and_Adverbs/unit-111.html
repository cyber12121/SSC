Unit 111 • Adjectives and adverbs

still, any more, yet, alreadystill (continuing action/state); not ... any more / any longer vs no longer; yet (in negatives and questions); already (sooner than expected)

'Still' shows a situation continues unchanged. 'Not ... any more' or 'no longer' indicates a situation has stopped. 'Yet' asks if something expected has happened, and 'already' expresses that something happened earlier than expected.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Use still to describe continuing situations and actions (still in bed, still working).
  • Express discontinued actions with not ... any more / any longer at the end of sentences, or no longer in mid-position.
  • Employ yet in negative sentences and questions to refer to things expected to happen (until now).
  • Use already for things that happened earlier than expected.
  • Contrast still ... not (surprise or impatience) with not ... yet (normal expectation).

Part A — Theory

Part A: Still

We use still to say that a situation or action is continuing. It hasn't changed or stopped:

It's ten o'clock and Joe is still in bed.
When I went to bed, Chris was still working.
Do you still want to go away or have you changed your mind?

Still also means ‘in spite of this’:

He has everything he needs, but he's still unhappy.

Still usually goes in the middle of the sentence with the verb (see Unit 110).

Part B: Any More / Any Longer / No Longer

We use not … any more or not … any longer to say that a situation has changed. Any more and any longer go at the end of a sentence:

Lucy doesn't work here any more. She left last month. (or ... any longer)
We used to be good friends, but we aren't any more. (or ... aren't any longer)

You can write any more (2 words) or anymore (1 word).

You can also use no longer. No longer goes in the middle of the sentence:

Lucy no longer works here.
We are no longer friends. (not we are no more friends)

Compare still and not … any more:

Sally still works here, but Lucy doesn't work here any more.

Part C: Yet

We use yet mainly in negative sentences (He isn't here yet) and questions (Is he here yet?). Yet (= until now) shows that the speaker expects something to happen.

Yet usually goes at the end of a sentence:

It's 10 o'clock and Joe isn't here yet.
Have you decided what to do yet?
‘Where are you going on holiday?’ ‘We don't know yet.’

We often use yet with the present perfect (Have you decided … yet?).

Compare yet and still:

Mike lost his job six months ago and is still unemployed.
Mike lost his job six months ago and hasn't found another job yet.
Is it still raining? • Has it stopped raining yet?

Still is also possible in negative sentences (before the negative) to express surprise or impatience:

She said she would be here an hour ago and she still hasn't come.
Compare: He hasn't replied yet. (I expect he will soon)
He still hasn't replied. (he should have replied before now – impatience)

Part D: Already

We use already to say that something happened sooner than expected:

‘What time is Sue leaving?’ ‘She has already left.’ (= sooner than you expected)
Shall I tell Joe what happened or does he already know?
I've just had lunch and I'm already hungry.

Already usually goes in the middle of a sentence or at the end:

She's already left. (or She's left already.)
