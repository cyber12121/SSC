Unit 87 • Pronouns and determiners

much, many, little, few, a lot, plentyquantifiers with countable & uncountable nouns; little vs a little, few vs a few

We use much and little with uncountable nouns, and many and few with plural countable nouns. 'A lot of' works with both. 'A little' and 'a few' indicate a positive quantity, while 'little' and 'few' mean not much/many.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Identify quantifiers used with uncountable nouns (much, little) vs plural countable nouns (many, few).
  • Use a lot of / lots of / plenty of correctly with both noun types.
  • Understand register constraints: much is unusual in affirmative spoken statements (use a lot of), but common with too, so, as.
  • Distinguish between little (almost none, negative) and a little (some, positive).
  • Distinguish between few (almost none, negative) and a few (a small number, positive).

Part A — Theory

Part A: Quantifiers with Countable vs Uncountable Nouns

We use much and little with uncountable nouns:

much luck • much time • little energy • little money

We use many and few with plural countable nouns:

many friends • many people • few cars • few children

We use a lot of / lots of / plenty of with both uncountable and plural nouns:

a lot of luck • lots of time • plenty of money (uncountable)
a lot of friends • lots of people • plenty of ideas (plural)

Plenty = more than enough:

There's no need to hurry. We've got plenty of time.
There's plenty to do in this town.

Part B: Much vs A Lot Of in Sentences

Much is unusual in positive sentences (especially in spoken English):

We didn't spend much money.
Do you see David much?

We spent a lot of money. (not We spent much money)
I see David a lot.

However, we use too much / so much / as much in positive sentences:

We spent too much money.
I love you so much.

We use many and a lot of in all kinds of sentences:

Many people drive too fast.  or  A lot of people …
Do you know many people?  or  … a lot of people?
There aren't many tourists here.  or  … a lot of tourists …

Note: We say many years / many weeks / many days (not usually a lot of years):

We've lived here for many years.

Part C: Little and Few (Negative Meaning = Not Much / Not Many)

Little = not much • Few = not many:

Gary is very busy with his job. He has little time for other things.
(= not much time, less than he would like)

Vicky doesn't like living in London. She has few friends there.
(= not many friends, not as many as she would like)

We often use very little and very few:

Gary has very little time for other things.
Vicky has very few friends in London.

Part D: A Little and A Few (Positive Meaning = Some)

A little = some, a small amount • A few = some, a small number:

Let's go and have coffee. We have a little time before the train leaves.
(= some time, enough time to have a coffee)

‘Do you speak English?’ ‘A little.’ (so we can communicate a bit)

I enjoy my life here. I have a few friends and we meet quite often.
(= some friends, enough to have a good time)

‘When was the last time you saw Clare?’ ‘A few days ago.’ (= 3 or 4 days ago)

Part E: Compare Little vs A Little • Few vs A Few

little / few (negative = almost none)

He spoke little English, so it was difficult to communicate with him.

She's lucky. She has few problems. (= almost no problems)

a little / a few (positive = some)

He spoke a little English, so we were able to communicate with him.

Things are not going so well. She has a few problems. (= some problems)

We say only a little (not only little) and only a few (not only few):

Hurry! We only have a little time.
The village was small. There were only a few houses.
