Unit 90 • Pronouns and determiners

all, every and wholeeverybody/everyone/everything vs all; whole vs all; every day vs all day

We use everybody/everyone/everything rather than 'all' alone for people and things. 'Whole' means complete/entire (mostly with singular countable nouns), while 'every' denotes repeated frequency.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  • Understand why everybody / everyone / everything is used rather than all alone as subject/object.
  • Use all in natural patterns: all + noun, all of us, we all ..., all about, all (= the only thing).
  • Use whole (= complete, entire) with singular countable nouns (the whole book, my whole life).
  • Contrast all day / the whole day (duration) with every day (frequency).
  • Treat every / everybody / everything as grammatically singular, with plural pronoun reference (they/them/their).

Part A — Theory

Part A: Everybody / Everyone / Everything vs All Alone

We say:

Everybody was happy.  or  Everyone was happy. (not all were happy)
He thinks he knows everything. (not knows all)
Our holiday was a disaster. Everything went wrong. (not all went wrong)

We do not often use all alone in this way. We use all in these structures:

• all + noun: All my friends were happy.
• all of + us/you/them: All of us were happy.
• we/you/they … all …: We were all happy.
• all about …: He knows all about computers.
• all … = the only thing(s): All I've eaten today is a banana.

Part B: Whole and All

Whole = complete, entire. We use whole mostly with singular countable nouns:

Did you read the whole book? (= all the book, not just part)
Emily has lived her whole life in the same town.
I ate a whole packet of biscuits.

We do not normally use whole with uncountable nouns:

Did you spend all the money I gave you? (not the whole money)
I read all the information carefully. (not the whole information)

Note: We use the / my / a before whole:

I read the whole book.
She worked the whole day.

I read all the information.
She worked all day. (not all the day)

Part C: Every Day vs All Day vs The Whole Day

We use every to indicate frequency (how often something happens):

We went to the beach every day. (not all days)
There's a bus every ten minutes.
We see each other about every six months.

All day or the whole day = duration (from beginning to end):

We spent all day on the beach.  or  We spent the whole day …
Dan was very quiet. He didn't say a word all evening.  or  … the whole evening.

Compare all the time and every time:

all the time (= continuously / always)

They never go out. They are at home all the time.

every time (= on each occasion)

Every time I see you, you look different.

Part D: Singular Verb with Every / Everyone

Every / everybody / everyone / everything are singular words, so they take a singular verb:

Every seat in the theatre was taken.
Everybody has arrived. (not have arrived)

We refer back to everybody / everyone using they / them / their:

Everybody said they enjoyed themselves. (= enjoyed himself or herself)
